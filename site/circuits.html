    Part I

    Logic

    Transistors as switches, switches as Boolean algebra, algebra as arithmetic.

    01
    Static CMOS logic

    Build a logic gate from switches

    Every static CMOS gate is two networks of switches: nFETs that pull the output to ground and pFETs that pull it to the supply, arranged so that exactly one of them conducts. Series nFETs make an AND, parallel ones an OR, and the pFETs are always the mirror image. Toggle the inputs: the simulator below finds which transistors conduct, which nodes are driven and where the current flows, just as a switch-level simulator in a chip company does for billions of transistors.

    The rules

    Sizing rule: every series path has the resistance of one unit nFET; pFETs are twice as wide for the same resistance (γ = 2). With it, the code reproduces Sutherland's textbook values (NAND2 g = 4/3, NOR2 g = 5/3, AOI21 p = 7/3), and the tests check every gate's truth table against its Boolean formula.

    Why it matters for transistors

    Series transistors are why NAND beats NOR in CMOS: stacked nFETs are cheap, stacked pFETs were slow, so designers built everything from NAND and inverting gates. FinFETs and nanosheets made pFETs nearly as strong as nFETs, but each extra input still adds a transistor to a stack, which is why real libraries stop at three or four inputs.

    02
    Arithmetic

    Add two numbers, one carry at a time

    Chain eight full adders and you can add two bytes. Each stage needs the carry from the stage before it, so in the worst case the answer ripples across the word one gate delay at a time, and the sum bits flicker through wrong values on the way (glitches, each one a wasted C·V²). This event-driven simulation uses gate delays from logical effort and the transistor model of the era you pick.

    The logic

    Gates switch with transport delays (every input change that changes the output propagates, so glitches are kept). Delays: XOR2 into one gate (h = 1), carry gate into the next carry and one XOR (h = 2), sum into four gates (h = 4). Tests check every 4-bit sum and that the worst case equals the formula.

    Why it matters for transistors

    The Intel 4004 (1971) added 4-bit numbers with about 2,300 transistors in total; a modern core has dozens of 64-bit adders, each built as a parallel-prefix tree that spends thousands of transistors to save tens of picoseconds. Cheap transistors are what made trading area for speed the right call, and glitch energy is one reason low-power designs still use simpler adders.

    Part II

    Memory

    Holding a bit while powered, and keeping it with the power off.

    03
    Six-transistor SRAM

    Store a bit with six transistors

    Two inverters feeding each other hold a 0 or a 1 for as long as the power is on; two access transistors let the bitlines read or overwrite it. Reading is the dangerous moment: the bitline pushes current into the node that holds 0 and lifts it. Plot one inverter against the mirror image of the other and you get the "butterfly"; the largest square that fits in its smaller wing is the noise the cell can survive.

    Butterfly curve

    Both inverter curves, one mirrored. The squares are the static noise margins of the two wings; the smaller one counts.

    Margin versus supply

    Hold and read noise margin as VDD is lowered. The open ring is your setting.

    Sixty times smaller, then stuck

    Published high-density six-transistor bitcell area by year. It fell about 2× per node until 5 nm, then barely moved until nanosheets (N2).

    Try this

    The physics

    CR: pull-down width over access width (cell ratio); PR: pull-up over access; β: pFET strength per width for the era. Rotating the plot by 45° turns "largest square" into "largest vertical gap" (Seevinck, 1987). Bitline: 256 cells, about 4 fF per µm of access width each; time shown is to develop 100 mV for the sense amplifier.

    Why it matters for transistors

    Caches take a large share of a processor's area, and the SRAM cell is the smallest thing a foundry can print reliably. Its six transistors must also be matched: random threshold mismatch, which grows as transistors shrink, eats the margin from one side. That is why bitcells stopped shrinking at 5 and 3 nm, and why cache-heavy chips are moving SRAM onto separate dies.

    04
    Floating-gate flash

    Keep a bit with the power off

    Bury a conductor inside the gate oxide, fully insulated, and any electrons you put on it stay for years. Getting them there takes a field of about 10 MV/cm across a few nanometres of oxide, enough for electrons to tunnel straight through (Fowler–Nordheim). The stored charge shifts the transistor's threshold, and reading the threshold reads the bit. Pack 2, 4, 8 or 16 threshold levels into the same window and one cell holds 1 to 4 bits.

    Incremental step pulse programming

    Each pulse is a little higher; after a few pulses the threshold climbs by one step per pulse until it passes the target.

    Threshold levels in one cell

    Each level is about one ISPP step wide plus noise. Dashed: read voltages. Red: overlap that a read cannot resolve.

    From one layer to 321

    When planar cells could not shrink further (a 20 nm cell stores only a few dozen electrons), NAND went vertical. Highest layer count in volume production, by year.

    Try this

    The physics

    Si/SiO₂ barrier φB = 3.1 eV, tunnelling mass 0.42 m₀ (Lenzlinger & Snow, 1969). Integrating in threshold rather than time keeps the solver stable across eleven decades of current. Tests check the FN slope, the one-step-per-pulse ISPP limit and that every extra bit shrinks the margin and lengthens the write.

    Why it matters for transistors

    Flash is a transistor that remembers. Kahng and Sze proposed the floating gate in 1967, Toshiba's Masuoka strung cells into NAND in 1987, and Samsung stacked 24 layers in 2013. Today's 3D NAND uses a charge-trapping nitride instead of a conductor, but the physics in this lab is the same, and so is the trade: more bits per cell means smaller margins, slower writes and fewer rewrite cycles.
