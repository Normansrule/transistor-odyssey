The transistor as an amplifier

Before transistors computed, they amplified: the first transistorized consumer product in the US was a hearing aid, and the first hit was a pocket radio. Every phone, radar and Wi-Fi chip still depends on transistors turning a faint signal into a strong one. Part I

Gain

A small change at the gate, a large change at the drain.

01
Common-source amplifier

Turn a small signal into a big one

Bias a transistor halfway on, hang a resistor from its drain to the supply, and wiggle the gate. The drain current follows the gate, the resistor turns that current into a voltage, and a millivolt in becomes many millivolts out, inverted. Push too hard and the output clips against the supply or ground: the curved transfer characteristic adds harmonics that were not in the input.

Transfer curve

Output voltage against input voltage. The dot is the bias point, the dashed line its slope (the gain), and the shaded bands the input and output swings.

Distortion versus input amplitude

Total harmonic distortion, harmonics 2–9. It rises with amplitude, then explodes when the output clips.

Try this

The physics

The output is the exact large-signal solution of the compact transistor model against the load line, not a linear approximation; the harmonics come from a discrete Fourier transform of one period. Tests check that the slope of the transfer curve equals −gm(r_o∥R_L) and that second-order distortion grows in proportion to amplitude.

Why it matters for transistors

The Regency TR-1, the first transistor radio sold in the US (1954), used four germanium junction transistors as amplifiers like this one. Today every radio receiver starts with such a stage, the low-noise amplifier, and every transmitter ends with one, the power amplifier, where linearity decides how much spectrum a signal pollutes.

02
Gain versus scaling

Why analog got harder as transistors shrank

A transistor’s intrinsic gain, gm·r_o, is the most voltage gain it can ever give. Long transistors have a large output resistance; short ones let the drain reach through and pull on the channel (drain-induced barrier lowering), so their output resistance and gain collapse. Transconductance efficiency gm/I_D says how much gain you buy per microampere: the analog designer’s exchange rate.

Transconductance efficiency

gm/I_D against current density for each generation: about 1/(nφ_t) in weak inversion, falling in strong inversion.

Intrinsic gain by generation

gm·r_o for every generation at the chosen current density and drain voltage.

Try this

The physics

gm and g_ds are numerical derivatives of the compact model at the chosen bias. In this model drain-induced barrier lowering (η) and channel-length modulation (λ) set g_ds. Tests check the weak-inversion limit of gm/I_D and that intrinsic gain drops from 180 nm to 45 nm planar and recovers with FinFETs.

Why it matters for transistors

Digital circuits only need a switch, but analog circuits need gain. As planar transistors shrank, intrinsic gain fell toward 10, and analog designers answered with longer channels, cascodes and digital calibration. FinFETs and nanosheets improved the gate’s grip on the channel and brought much of the gain back.

Part II

Speed and noise

How fast a transistor can amplify, and the faintest signal it can hear.

03
Frequency response

How fast can it amplify?

Every transistor has capacitance, and every amplifier must charge it. The gate-to-drain capacitance is the worst: the stage’s own gain multiplies it at the input (the Miller effect), so a fast transistor can still make a slow amplifier. The transit frequency f_T, where the current gain falls to one, is the speed limit of the device itself.

Bode plot

Gain and phase of the common-source stage, including the Miller-multiplied input capacitance and the right-half-plane zero.

The fastest transistors

Record f_T and f_max by technology (room temperature unless marked), and this model’s intrinsic peak f_T by CMOS generation.

Try this

The physics

Two-pole model of the common-source stage with a source resistance R_S and a load capacitance C_L. C_gs = ⅔C_oxWL plus overlap; C_gd is the overlap and fringe capacitance (0.25 fF/µm). f_T is intrinsic: wiring adds more capacitance in a real layout. Tests check the Miller capacitance and that the input or output pole dominates in the right limits.

Why it matters for transistors

In 1954 the TR-1 amplified AM broadcasts below 2 MHz. Today silicon CMOS amplifies near 80 GHz in car radars, and indium phosphide transistors amplify at 1 THz. Every step up in frequency opened a new use: FM, television, mobile phones, satellite links, Wi-Fi, 5G millimetre wave and sub-terahertz 6G research.

04
Noise

The floor under every signal

Electrons jiggle with heat, so every transistor adds a little random voltage of its own. Thermal noise is flat across frequency and falls as you spend more current on gm. At low frequency a second kind rises as 1/f, from charges trapped and released at the oxide interface, and it is worse in small transistors. Below the noise floor a signal is lost.

Noise spectrum

Input-referred noise density. Thermal noise is flat; flicker noise rises as 1/f; the corner is where they cross.

Bigger transistors are quieter

Integrated noise from 10 Hz to the chosen bandwidth against device width, at fixed gm.

Try this

The physics

γ = 2/3 is the long-channel value (short channels are higher); K_f = 10⁻²⁵ V²F is a teaching value of the right order for silicon CMOS. Tests check that the two terms are equal at the corner, that the closed-form rms matches numerical integration, and that four times the area gives a four-times lower corner.

Why it matters for transistors

Noise sets the range of every radio, the resolution of every sensor and the energy of every analog-to-digital converter. Low-noise amplifiers for radio astronomy and quantum computers use indium phosphide HEMTs cooled to a few kelvin, because thermal noise falls with temperature.
