How to read an energy band diagram

A band diagram plots the energy an electron would need at each point in a device. Four rules are enough to read every diagram on this page.

1 · Two edges and a gap

Free electrons live at or just above the conduction-band edge Ec (blue, filled dots). Missing electrons, called holes, live at or just below the valence-band edge Ev (orange, open dots). In between is the band gap, where an electron cannot sit: 1.12 eV in silicon, 3.4 eV in gallium nitride (GaN), 5.47 eV in diamond.

2 · The Fermi level is the water line

The dashed amber line, the Fermi level EF, marks how full the states are. Where Ec comes close to EF there are many electrons; where Ev comes close there are many holes. With no bias it is flat everywhere. Applying a voltage V splits it by qV between the two contacts, and that split is what drives a current.

3 · Electrons roll down, holes float up

A slope in the bands is an electric field. Electrons slide downhill on Ec like marbles, and holes rise on Ev like bubbles. Energy increases upward for electrons, so a hole must be pushed down to gain energy. Every animation below moves its carriers by these rules.

4 · A transistor is a barrier the gate can move

Almost every device here works the same way: carriers must climb a barrier between source and drain, and the fraction that make it falls by e (about 2.7×) for every kT, or 26 meV at room temperature, of height. The gate lowers the barrier; this gives the famous 60 mV-per-decade limit. The tunnel FET and Schottky-barrier nanotube FET cheat by tunnelling through instead.

    Builder · Anderson's rule + Poisson solver

    Put two materials together

    When two semiconductors meet, the step in the conduction band (ΔEc) and in the valence band (ΔEv) decides where electrons and holes collect. That step is why an aluminium gallium nitride / gallium nitride (AlGaN/GaN) stack traps a sheet of electrons, why a silicon-germanium (SiGe) base speeds up a bipolar transistor, and why hafnium oxide leaks more than silicon dioxide.
