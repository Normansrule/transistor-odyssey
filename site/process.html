    Part I

    Front end

    Building the transistor itself: oxides and doped regions in the silicon.

    01
    Thermal oxidation

    Grow an oxide, one nanometre at a time

    Heat silicon in oxygen or steam and it grows the near-perfect insulator that made the MOSFET possible. Oxygen has to diffuse through the oxide already there before it can react, so growth starts fast (limited by the reaction at the interface) and then slows down (limited by diffusion). The Deal–Grove model from 1965 captures both regimes in one quadratic, and it is still the starting point of every process simulator.

    Thickness versus time

    Log–log: slope 1 is reaction-limited (linear), slope ½ is diffusion-limited (parabolic). The dot is your setting.

    Arrhenius plot of the rate constants

    B (parabolic) and B/A (linear) for both ambients against 1000/T; the slopes are the activation energies.

    Try this

    The physics

    Rate constants for (111) silicon at 1 atm (Deal and Grove, tabulated by Hollauer); (100) silicon divides B/A by 1.68. Both scale linearly with pressure. Dry oxidation starts from a τ equivalent to 25 nm, because the first few tens of nanometres grow faster than the model predicts. Tests check the linear and parabolic limits and recover the 1.23 eV activation energy.

    Why it matters for transistors

    Silicon owes its dominance to this oxide. Gate oxides fell from 120 nm in 1971 to 1.2 nm in 2005, grown in seconds at the bottom of the linear regime, until tunnelling forced the switch to hafnium oxide (Physics Lab 07). Thick wet oxides still isolate devices and protect surfaces in power and analog chips.

    02
    Ion implantation and diffusion

    Fire dopants into the crystal, then bake them in

    Every source, drain and well is made by accelerating ionised boron, phosphorus or arsenic to tens of kiloelectronvolts and firing them into the wafer. Each ion zig-zags through the lattice, losing energy to atomic nuclei and to the electron cloud, and stops at a depth set by its mass and energy. An anneal then repairs the damage and lets the dopants diffuse, which sets the junction depth.

    Dopant profile

    Concentration versus depth: dashed as implanted, solid after the anneal. The flat line is the background doping and the marker is the junction.

    Projected range versus energy

    LSS theory for four dopants in silicon. Circles mark where electronic stopping overtakes nuclear stopping.

    Try this

    The physics

    Nuclear stopping uses the ZBL universal curve and electronic stopping the Lindhard k√E law. The ranges land within about 20 % of tabulated values (B at 80 keV: 255 nm against 240 nm). Diffusivities are intrinsic values from the Illinois table; transient-enhanced and concentration-dependent diffusion are left out. Sheet resistance integrates Caughey–Thomas mobility over the layer.

    Why it matters for transistors

    Scaling shrank junctions as fast as gates: from micrometres in the 1970s to about 10 nm today, made with sub-keV implants and millisecond laser or flash anneals so the dopants barely move. In nanosheet transistors the source and drain are now grown already doped (in-situ epitaxy), but implantation still makes the wells, the threshold adjustment and the halo pockets that fight short-channel effects.

    Part II

    The fab as a business

    Why the cost of a transistor stopped falling.

    03
    Die yield and cost

    Count the dies that survive

    A 300 mm wafer goes through about a thousand steps, and a single particle in the wrong place kills a die. Bigger dies catch more defects, so yield falls with area, and defects cluster, which helps. Multiply by the wafer price of each node and you get the cost of a working chip, and the reason the cost per transistor stopped falling around 28 nm.

    Poisson, Murphy and negative binomial at your defect density. The dot is your die; the open ring is this wafer’s Monte Carlo yield.

    Cost of the same chip, node by node

    The physics

    The wafer map keeps only dies fully inside a 3 mm edge exclusion. Defects are a Poisson process whose rate on each die is gamma-distributed (shape α), which reproduces the negative-binomial yield on average; a test checks the Monte Carlo against the formula. Wafer prices for N3 and N2 are press reports.

    Why it matters for transistors

    From 90 nm to 28 nm the same chip got about 7 times cheaper. From 7 nm to 5 nm it got no cheaper at all, because wafer prices rose as fast as density. That is why chiplets exist: four 150 mm² dies yield far better than one 600 mm² die, and only the logic that needs the newest node pays for it.

    Part III

    Back end

    The copper wiring above the transistors, and how it wears out.

    04
    Electromigration

    Watch a wire tear itself apart

    At a million amperes per square centimetre, the stream of electrons in a copper line pushes on the metal atoms hard enough to move them. Atoms leave the cathode end and pile up at the anode, building stress until a void opens and the wire fails. A short wire can hold the stress back and never fail; a long one always fails, eventually. This lab solves the stress equation along the wire.

    Lifetime versus line length

    Simulated time to a void at your current and temperature. Below the Blech length the wire is immortal.

    Black’s law

    Lifetime of a long line versus current density at four temperatures: slope −2 on a log–log plot.

    Try this

    The physics

    Korhonen’s equation with blocked ends, solved implicitly on an adaptive grid. Teaching parameters for copper with a dielectric cap: Eₐ = 0.9 eV, Z* = 5 and σ_crit = 300 MPa, giving (jL)_crit ≈ 3,900 A/cm. Tests check the linear steady state, the Blech threshold, and that the solver reproduces Black’s exponent n = 2.

    Why it matters for transistors

    Wires shrink with transistors but current does not shrink as fast, so current density keeps rising. Electromigration has set the maximum current in every design rule since the aluminium era, drove the switch to copper in 1997, and is one reason the newest nodes look at ruthenium and cobalt (Physics Lab 15) and at short, Blech-immortal wire segments.
