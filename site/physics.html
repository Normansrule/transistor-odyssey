Part I

Materials

What a semiconductor is: atoms, bands, carriers.

01
Crystal structure

The lattices behind the materials

The band gap, the mobility and the polarization that makes GaN transistors work all start with where the atoms sit. Drag to rotate, scroll to zoom, hover an atom.

Try this

The physics

Positions are generated in Python from the lattice constants and exported to the page; bonds are drawn between nearest neighbours.

Why it matters for transistors

Silicon, germanium and diamond share the diamond-cubic lattice; GaAs adds two species (zincblende); GaN and SiC stack hexagonal layers, which breaks the symmetry and creates built-in polarization; MoS₂ is a layered crystal that can be peeled to one molecule thick.

02
Band structure

Where band gaps come from

Put an electron in a perfectly periodic crystal and only certain energies can travel through it. The Kronig–Penney model solves this exactly for a row of square barriers: slide the barrier height and watch allowed bands and forbidden gaps form, and the electron's effective mass change.

Crystal potential and allowed bands | E(k) dispersion

Left: four unit cells with the allowed energy bands shaded. Right: the same bands as energy versus crystal momentum; the dashed curve is a free electron folded into the zone.

The Bloch condition f(E) = cos(kd)

An energy is allowed only where f(E) lies between −1 and +1. Hover to read f(E).

Try this

The physics

For E > V₀, β becomes imaginary and cosh, sinh turn into cos, sin. The effective mass is read from the curvature of band 1 at its bottom; a test checks it equals the free mass when V₀ → 0.

Why it matters for transistors

Band gaps, effective masses and the difference between metals, semiconductors and insulators all come from this picture. Real crystals are three-dimensional and need methods like tight-binding or density-functional theory, but every one of them reduces to Bloch's theorem as shown here.

Python reference: sim/transistor_sim/physics/bandstructure.py · browser twin checked against it in CI.

03
Bands & carriers

Where the electrons are

A semiconductor's electrons live in energy bands separated by a forbidden gap. Temperature kicks a few across the gap; dopants add many more. Change the material, heat it, dope it, and watch the Fermi level and the carriers respond.

The Fermi level is within 3kT of a band edge: the material is degenerate. Boltzmann statistics, and this model, are no longer accurate.

Intrinsic density vs temperature, all six materials

Once ni passes the doping line, the material stops behaving as doped. Hover to compare.

Try this

The physics

Varshni's empirical fit for the gap; effective densities of states Nc, Nv scale as T3/2; every dopant is assumed ionized.

Why it matters for transistors

The whole transistor rests on n ≫ ni: dopants, not heat, must decide where the carriers are. That is why silicon chips throttle near 100–125 °C, why SiC and GaN run power converters at 200 °C and above, and why diamond is studied for extreme environments.

Python reference: sim/transistor_sim/physics/carriers.py · browser twin checked against it in CI.

Part II

Junctions and gates

The building blocks of every transistor.

04
pn junction

The junction under every transistor

Put p-type and n-type silicon together and carriers diffuse across, leaving behind a depletion region of fixed ions with a strong built-in field. Every source and drain is a pn junction; so is every solar cell and LED.

Charge → field → bands

Poisson's equation, integrated twice. Hover for values.

Current–voltage (100 µm × 100 µm diode)

Ideal Shockley diode, log scale.

Try this

The physics

Depletion approximation: the junction region is fully emptied of mobile carriers, the rest is neutral. I0 uses Caughey–Thomas mobilities and 1 µs lifetimes.

Why it matters for transistors

In a MOSFET the source and drain junctions must stay reverse-biased and leak as little as possible; their depletion regions are what the drain uses to reach under the gate in short channels (Lab 09). In power devices the same equations set breakdown voltage.

Python reference: sim/transistor_sim/physics/junction.py · browser twin checked against it in CI.

05
MOS capacitor

How a gate makes a channel

Metal, oxide, semiconductor: the heart of the MOSFET without its source and drain. A gate voltage bends the bands at the silicon surface until the surface inverts and fills with electrons. This is an exact solution, not the depletion approximation.

Band diagram: gate | oxide | silicon

Electron energy; the silicon Fermi level EF is the zero. The oxide band tilts with the voltage across it.

Capacitance–voltage

The measurement every fab runs on test capacitors.

Carriers vs depth

Electrons and holes follow the band bending exponentially.

Try this

The physics

β = q/kT and φB = (kT/q) ln(NA/ni). The band profile comes from integrating dψ/dx = −(√2 kT/qLD) F(ψ) into the bulk.

Why it matters for transistors

Threshold voltage, gate capacitance and the inversion charge that carries current all come from this picture. Scaling meant raising Cox (thinner, then high-k oxides) and, once channels became undoped fins and sheets, setting VT with the gate metal's work function.

Python reference: sim/transistor_sim/physics/moscap.py · browser twin checked against it in CI.

From capacitor to transistor

The MOSFET from first principles

Add a source and drain to the MOS capacitor and current flows through the inversion layer. The charge-sheet model uses the exact surface potential of Lab 05 at each end of the channel, so one formula covers subthreshold diffusion, the linear region and saturation — no fitting parameters.

The physics

The first bracket is drift, the second diffusion. ψs0 and ψsL are the surface potentials at the source (V = 0) and the drain (V = VDS). Long channel, constant mobility: the textbook starting point before short-channel effects (Lab 09) and velocity saturation (Lab 10).

Why it matters for transistors

Every compact model used to design chips (BSIM, PSP) descends from this surface-potential picture. Tests check that it reproduces the linear-region transconductance, the square law in saturation and n·60 mV/decade below threshold.

Part III

Quantum effects

What changes when devices reach a few nanometres.

07
Quantum tunnelling

Why oxides can't get thinner

An electron is a wave. Meet a barrier thinner than its decay length and part of the wave leaks through. The transfer-matrix method solves the Schrödinger equation exactly for any stack of flat slabs; the dots show the textbook formula agreeing.

Transmission vs energy

Solid: transfer matrix. Circles: closed form (single barrier).

Gate-oxide tunnelling at equal EOT

Trapezoidal barrier, 1 V across the stack, electron at the silicon band edge.

Try this

The physics

Matching ψ and ψ′/m* at every interface gives a 2×2 matrix per interface; their product yields T. The code multiplies interface determinants exactly so T stays accurate down to 10⁻³⁰.

Why it matters for transistors

By the 90–65 nm nodes, SiO₂ gates were ~1.2 nm thick and leaked amperes per square centimetre. Intel's 2007 switch to HfO₂ gave the same capacitance with a physically thicker film. The same physics powers flash memory, tunnel FETs and the Esaki diode.

Python reference: sim/transistor_sim/physics/tunnel.py · browser twin checked against it in CI.

08
Quantum wells

When a channel gets too thin to be classical

Squeeze electrons into a layer a few nanometres thick and their energy comes in discrete steps. Solve the Schrödinger equation for a silicon nanosheet between oxide walls, or solve Schrödinger and Poisson together for the two-dimensional electron gas that makes GaN transistors work.

Potential, energy levels and wavefunctions

Each wavefunction is drawn at the height of its energy level.

Sweep

Nanosheet: ground-state energy versus thickness. GaN: sheet density versus barrier thickness, self-consistent against the analytic formula.

Try this

The physics

Finite differences with the BenDaniel–Duke mass boundary condition. The 2DEG is solved self-consistently: each step solves Schrödinger, then a nonlinear Poisson equation by Newton's method with the electron density allowed to respond to the potential (Trellakis predictor–corrector). It converges in 10–40 steps.

Why it matters for transistors

Production nanosheets are about 5 nm thick, so confinement already raises the lowest level by ~15 meV and shifts the threshold voltage. In GaN HEMTs the whole channel is a quantum well: its density, set by polarization and barrier thickness, fixes the current a power or RF transistor can carry.

Python reference: sim/transistor_sim/physics/qwell.py · browser twin checked against it in CI.

Part IV

Scaling and transport

Why transistors changed shape, and their speed and heat limits.

09
Short-channel electrostatics

Why the FinFET had to happen

A live two-dimensional Poisson solve of a transistor in its off state, 81 × up to 110 grid points relaxed by successive over-relaxation every time you move a slider. Shrink the gate and the drain's field reaches in and lowers the source barrier.

Conduction band along the leakiest path

The barrier electrons must climb to leave the source.

DIBL vs gate length

Swept in a background worker for your body and oxide thickness.

Try this

The physics

N = 1 for a single gate, 2 for a double gate; the ideal swing is ≈ 60 mV/dec at 300 K. The gate, source and drain are fixed-potential boundaries; oxide side walls carry no flux. Off-current is estimated from the barrier along each horizontal path, I ∝ Σ exp(−Eb/kT).

Why it matters for transistors

The rule of thumb L ≳ 5–6 λ is the reason gate geometry changed. Planar bulk ran out of λ near 20 nm; fins (two or three sides), then nanosheets (four sides) and thinner bodies each cut λ so gates could keep shrinking.

Python reference: sim/transistor_sim/physics/poisson2d.py · browser twin checked against it in CI.

10
Hot-electron transport

Why electrons have a speed limit

Five hundred electrons in a silicon crystal, each flying freely under the field and then scattering off lattice vibrations. This is ensemble Monte Carlo, the method behind industrial device simulators, running live. Every flash is a collision.

Velocity vs field

Your steady-state runs against the measured curve.

Mean velocity over time

Step the field to see velocity overshoot.

Try this

The physics

Single isotropic valley, m* = 0.26 m0, ħω = 63 meV, α = 0.5 eV⁻¹. Two coupling constants are calibrated to silicon's measured µ ≈ 1400 cm²/V·s and vsat ≈ 10⁷ cm/s.

Why it matters for transistors

In a 2 nm-class transistor the channel field is well past 10⁴ V/cm, so electrons run near saturation velocity. Drive current then depends on vsat and on how far electrons get before they scatter, which is why strain, SiGe and III–V channels were pursued.

Python reference: sim/transistor_sim/physics/montecarlo.py · browser twin checked against it in CI.

11
Self-heating

Where the watts go

A GaN power amplifier dissipates several watts per millimetre of gate in a strip about a micrometre wide. This lab solves the 2D heat equation under that hot spot and shows how much the substrate beneath it — sapphire, silicon, SiC or diamond — decides how hot the transistor runs.

Channel above ~175 °C: GaN reliability drops quickly, and real thermal conductivity falls with temperature, so the true peak would be higher still.

Surface temperature

Temperature rise along the top surface, log distance from the gate.

Same device, four substrates

Peak temperature rise at your power, heater and boundary settings.

Try this

The physics

Finite volumes on a grid graded from 1/8 of the heater width up to tens of micrometres; the heat-sink face is fixed at 300 K. The block-tridiagonal system is solved directly. A test checks the exact 1D limit, boundary resistance included.

Why it matters for transistors

Output power in RF GaN is limited by temperature long before voltage or current run out. That is why GaN is grown on SiC, why GaN-on-diamond is under development, and why chip designers spread fingers apart. The same heat-spreading physics now dominates 3D-stacked logic and AI accelerators.

Manufacturing

How the pattern gets onto the wafer.

12
Lithography optics

How light prints a 28 nm pitch

The mask diffracts light into orders; the lens can only catch the ones inside its pupil. Change the wavelength, the numerical aperture, the pitch and the shape of the light source, and see which orders get through, what image reaches the resist, and what actually prints.

Aerial image

Light intensity on the wafer across two pitches.

Contrast versus pitch

Coherent, conventional and dipole illumination for this scanner.

Try this

The physics

Abbe's method: each source point images the mask coherently and the intensities add. Defocus adds a phase πλz(f² − fs²) to each order. Tests check the λ/NA coherent cut-off and that nothing prints below λ/2NA.

Why it matters for transistors

Every transistor on a chip is defined by an image like this one. The whole history of shrinking — shorter wavelengths, immersion (NA above 1), off-axis illumination, EUV and High-NA EUV — is a fight to get the first diffraction order through the lens.

Part VI

From transistor to circuit

What one switch means for a chip: logic, the speed limit and the wires.

13
CMOS inverter

From one transistor to a logic gate

Put an nFET and a pFET in series between the supply and ground, tie their gates together, and you have the inverter: the circuit behind every logic gate and every SRAM cell. This lab builds it from the same compact transistor model as the history page, then measures what a chip designer cares about: where it switches, how much noise it tolerates, how fast it is and how much energy each switch costs.

Voltage transfer curve

Output versus input, noise margins shaded. Dashed: the same curve mirrored, as two inverters in a loop form an SRAM cell ("butterfly").

Speed and energy across generations

Each era at its own supply, fan-out of four; the open ring is your current settings.

Try this

The physics

The load is a fan-out of identical inverters (gate capacitance Cox·L per µm of width) plus 0.6 fF/µm of contacts and local wire. Delays are for an ideal input step; real fan-out-of-4 delays are longer because inputs ramp. Tests check VM = VDD/2 for matched devices, E ∝ VDD², and the constant-current limit of the delay.

Why it matters for transistors

Every scaling generation was ultimately judged by this circuit: shorter gate delay, lower energy per switch, more gain to restore noisy signals. Drain-induced barrier lowering softens the transfer curve, which is part of why FinFETs were needed, and the energy C·V² is why supply voltage stopped falling only when the threshold voltage could not follow it.

Python reference: sim/transistor_sim/physics/inverter.py · browser twin checked against it in CI.

14
Ballistic transport

How fast can a transistor possibly be?

In a 20 nm channel an electron may cross without scattering at all. The top-of-barrier model treats the transistor as a source that injects electrons over a barrier at their thermal velocity, a drain that injects some back, and a channel that reflects a fraction of them. It gives the absolute upper limit on current, and shows how close real silicon, InGaAs, germanium and MoS₂ devices get to it.

Output characteristics

With scattering (solid) and in the ballistic limit (dashed).

How ballistic is it?

Current as a fraction of the ballistic limit versus channel length, at your gate and drain voltages, each material at its own mobility.

Try this

The physics

ηS = (EF − Etop)/kT is solved self-consistently with the gate electrostatics by bisection; ℓ is the "kT layer" just past the top of the barrier. Intrinsic device only: no series resistance. Tests check vT ≈ 1.2×10⁷ cm/s for silicon, I = q ns vT in the nondegenerate ballistic limit and saturation within a few kT/q.

Why it matters for transistors

Mobility stops being the whole story below about 50 nm: what matters is how fast electrons are injected at the top of the barrier and how few scatter back in the first few nanometres. That is why strain still helps (lighter mass, higher vT), why III-V channels looked promising but ran into the density-of-states bottleneck, and why source resistance, not the channel, now limits drive current.

Python reference: sim/transistor_sim/physics/ballistic.py · browser twin checked against it in CI.

15
Interconnect

Why the wires are now slower than the transistors

A chip has tens of kilometres of copper wiring in up to twenty layers. As the lowest lines shrink below copper's 40 nm electron mean free path, electrons scatter off surfaces and grain boundaries and the barrier around each line eats into the copper. This lab computes the resistance, capacitance and delay of a line and shows why chipmakers are turning to ruthenium, cobalt and tungsten.

Line resistance versus width

Four metals with size effects; copper carries its barrier (set in the panel), the others a 0.3 nm adhesion layer.

Delay versus length

Unbuffered wire, optimally repeated wire, and one gate delay.

Try this

The physics

Surface scattering in the wire form of Fuchs–Sondheimer (p = 0, C = 1.2) plus Mayadas–Shatzkes grain-boundary scattering (R = 0.4, grains as large as the line is narrow), with bulk mean free paths from Gall (2016). The animation solves the RC diffusion equation by Crank–Nicolson; a test checks it against the exact erfc solution.

Why it matters for transistors

Since the 130 nm node, wire delay and wire energy have grown while transistor delay shrank. Low-k dielectrics, air gaps, thinner barriers, backside power delivery and new metals are all attempts to win back the RC product. The transistor is no longer the slowest part of a chip; the wires that connect them are.
